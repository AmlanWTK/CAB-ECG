## 1 Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [@singer2016]. It remains a major cause of death worldwide: the Global Burden of Disease study estimated 48.9 million cases and 11.0 million sepsis-related deaths in 2017, almost a fifth of all deaths that year [@rudd2020]. Treatment is time-critical. In a statewide study of mandated emergency sepsis care in New York, each additional hour taken to complete the initial treatment bundle was associated with higher in-hospital mortality [@seymour2017]. Identifying patients at risk early, before organ dysfunction is established, is therefore an important opportunity for clinical decision support.

Early-warning models address this need by estimating each patient's risk at regular intervals, typically every hour, from routinely charted electronic health record (EHR) data such as vital signs and laboratory results, and by raising an alert when the risk score exceeds a threshold. Many hospitals do not develop such models themselves. They obtain them from EHR vendors or specialised providers, or adopt models published by other institutions. The model is then deployed with an alert threshold that was chosen on the developer's patients, although the receiving hospital may differ in its patients, its clinical practice and its tolerance for alerts.

Predictive performance is not necessarily preserved when a model moves between institutions. Differences in patient populations, measurement practices, documentation and outcome definitions produce dataset shift, which can degrade performance at the receiving site [@finlayson2021; @subbaswamy2020]. Sepsis models are particularly exposed. Many of their inputs, such as serum lactate, are available only when a clinician orders the test, so their presence reflects local practice as well as the patient's condition, and the outcome itself is defined from locally recorded data. An independent external validation of a widely implemented proprietary sepsis model illustrates the consequences: it reported an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.63, against the developer's reported 0.76–0.83, and the model failed to identify two thirds of the patients with sepsis [@wong2021].

Performance can also degrade in different ways. A transferred model may lose sensitivity and fail to alert on patients who develop sepsis, or it may generate many more false alerts among patients who do not, adding to clinical workload and alarm fatigue. These failure modes have different clinical consequences, and Section 4 shows that the same two hospitals can produce either one, depending on which hospital supplies the model and which receives it. A summary measure such as accuracy or AUROC therefore cannot establish whether a model meets the receiving hospital's requirements. The deployment decision has to be made against those requirements directly.

A receiving hospital that can label a sample of its own patients has four options, in order of increasing intervention:

- **keep** the imported model and its threshold unchanged;
- **calibrate** it, which in this chapter means only moving the alert threshold, without retraining the model or rescaling its predicted risks;
- **adapt** it, by continuing its training on local patients;
- **block** it, so that the model is not deployed and usual care continues.

These options correspond to established strategies for updating clinical prediction models [@steyerberg2019; @vancalster2019]. Closed testing procedures select among updating strategies with significance tests of model fit [@vergouwe2017], and sample-size guidance determines how many patients are needed to estimate performance with a given precision [@riley2021]. These methods provide guarantees for their own objectives, but estimating performance precisely and establishing that a model meets predefined safety requirements are distinct goals. None of them bounds the probability that the configuration finally deployed violates the hospital's sensitivity or false-alert requirement. A point estimate from a few hundred local patients can look acceptable when the true performance is not, and a test of fit can favour a model that still misses too many septic patients. A deployment procedure must decide not only which option to use, but also whether the available evidence justifies deploying any of them.

This chapter introduces CAB (Calibrate, Adapt, or Block), a deployment gate that makes this decision with a finite-sample statistical guarantee. Before any local outcome label is examined, the hospital specifies two requirements: a minimum patient-level sensitivity, and a maximum false-alert rate among patients who do not develop sepsis. CAB considers the options in order of increasing intervention and deploys the first configuration whose compliance with both requirements is certified by exact binomial tests on labelled local patients. An adapted model is fitted on one half of the labelled patients and certified on the other, so that no patient is used both to train and to certify it. If no configuration is certified, the model is blocked. The tests are arranged so that the probability of deploying a configuration that violates either requirement is at most a level α chosen in advance (α = 0.05 in the main analysis). This bound holds for any shift between the developing and the receiving hospital and for any number of labels, provided that the labelled patients are a random sample of the receiving hospital's patients and that their labels are correct. CAB builds on the fixed-sequence testing principle of Learn-then-Test risk control [@angelopoulos2021; @bates2021], and applies it to the ordered options available to a hospital and to patient-level alerting requirements.

Two clarifications define the scope of this guarantee. First, a block is not a finding that the model is unsafe: it means that the labelled patients do not provide enough evidence to certify any configuration, which happens both when the model is unsafe and when it is safe but the labels are too few to show it. Second, certification concerns the two stated requirements at the time of deployment. It does not demonstrate clinical benefit, and it does not cover changes in the patient population after deployment.

CAB is evaluated on the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2019 data [@reyna2020], which comprise 40,336 intensive care patients from two US hospitals. The six transfer scenarios comprise two whole-hospital transfers, in which the model developed at each hospital is imported into the other, and four transfers into a single ICU type, in which each model is imported into the other hospital's medical or surgical ICU. A model imported into its own hospital serves as a control in which nothing has shifted. CAB is compared with eleven alternatives over local samples of 250 to 12,000 labelled patients, nine pairs of safety requirements and 100 repetitions per setting. The labelled patients are used only by the procedures being compared; each resulting deployment is then judged on the receiving hospital's remaining patients, which were not labelled.

The chapter makes five contributions:

1. **A certified deployment gate.** CAB chooses among keeping, calibrating, adapting and blocking an imported model, with a finite-sample bound on the probability of deploying a configuration that violates either requirement, and without a model of the shift (Sections 3.4 and 3.5).
2. **A comparison with eleven alternatives** drawn from current practice and the literature, including classical closed-testing updating, point-estimate local validation, prevalence correction and Learn-then-Test without CAB's order (Section 4).
3. **A closed-form label calculator** that estimates, before any labelling, how many septic and non-septic patients certification will require (Section 4.5).
4. **Practical findings for hospitals and vendors.** Blocking was frequent, both when no configuration met the requirements and when the labelled patients were too few to certify one that did; samples of a few hundred labelled patients rarely allowed any configuration to be certified, even where a safe one existed; and the number of labels needed to approve an unchanged model was determined by the hospital's false-alert tolerance rather than by the vendor's threshold headroom (Sections 4.2, 4.5 and 4.7).
5. **A prototype application** that runs the gate on a hospital's labelled patients and then monitors patients hourly with the configuration it deployed, sharing its code with the experiments (Section 5).

The rest of the chapter is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews sepsis early warning, model updating, distribution-free risk control and the regulatory context. Section 3 describes the data, the safety requirements, the CAB procedure and its guarantee, the comparators and the experimental design. Section 4 reports the results, Section 5 presents the prototype application, Section 6 discusses the implications and limitations, and Section 7 concludes.
