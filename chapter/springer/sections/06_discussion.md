## 6 Discussion

### 6.1 Principal Findings

Three findings stand out, and they rest on different kinds of evidence. The first is empirical: sepsis models transferred between two hospitals violated the receiving hospital's requirements in ways that depended on the direction of transfer. One model lost sensitivity; the other alerted two thirds of the patients who were not septic. A single summary measure such as AUROC would not have revealed which requirement was broken.

The second is also empirical: the usual ways of handling an imported model deployed configurations that violated the requirements in most runs, whether the model was used as supplied, given a new threshold, retrained or updated by classical closed testing. Many of these were silent failures, in which the hospital's own labelled patients suggested that the configuration was acceptable. Point-estimate validation, the closest analogue of current local validation, was unsafe in about a third of runs, and in our largest budgets it still deployed unsafe configurations, for example in 29% of runs for B→A with 12,000 labels. Although larger samples improve estimation, they do not by themselves give a point-estimate decision rule an explicit finite-sample bound on the probability of an unsafe deployment.

The third combines a theoretical guarantee with empirical support. Under the assumptions of Section 3.5, CAB bounds by α the probability, over repeated labelled samples, of deploying a configuration that violates either requirement. The bound does not require the developing and receiving hospitals to have the same patient distribution, but it depends on the validity of the receiving hospital's sampling and certification procedure. In every setting we examined, at three error levels, the observed unsafe-deployment rates were consistent with that bound. Throughout, an unsafe-deployment rate is the share of all runs, with blocked runs counted as safe, because that is the probability the guarantee bounds; among only the runs in which something was deployed, the share is necessarily higher. The price is explicit: certification needs enough labelled patients to demonstrate safety, and when they are not available CAB blocks.

These findings should be kept distinct from what a single deployment shows. The theorem describes the procedure's behaviour over repeated labelled samples; a certification decision is the outcome of the exact tests on one hospital's sample; and the retrospective evaluations in Sections 4 and 5 describe how the deployed configurations performed on patients the gate did not use. None of them, on its own, shows that deploying the model improves patient outcomes.

### 6.2 What the Guarantee Covers

The guarantee is a statement about the deployment decision, under three assumptions. The labelled patients must be a random sample of the patients the model will serve, or a sample drawn at random within the septic and non-septic groups. The labels, including onset times, must be correct. And the requirements, the imported model and the vendor's threshold must be fixed before labelling, with the adapted model depending only on the fit half.

Each assumption has practical consequences. Labelling only the patients clinicians flagged, or only those admitted in a period that differs from the deployment period, breaks the first. Retrospective sepsis labels derived from the record, such as the Challenge's, can be wrong or mistimed. In our limited label-noise experiment, missed sepsis labels resulted in fewer deployments rather than an observed increase in unsafe deployments; this does not establish robustness to other forms of label error, including false-positive labels or inaccurate onset times. The third assumption is a matter of governance: a hospital that adjusts its requirements after seeing the labels, or a vendor that tunes the model on the receiving hospital's data, removes the basis for the bound.

The guarantee also has a fixed scope in time. It covers the moment of deployment, not the period after it. Changes in the patient population, clinical practice or documentation after deployment can invalidate assumptions that held at go-live, so a configuration certified then can drift out of compliance. Relevant regulatory frameworks address lifecycle oversight, controlled changes to AI-enabled systems and, where applicable, post-market monitoring [@fda2024; @euaiact2024]; the obligations that apply to a particular deployment depend on the system and its regulatory context.

### 6.3 Implications for Hospitals and Vendors

**Planning for blocking.** Blocking was the appropriate decision under CAB's rule for one direction of transfer at the primary requirements, and whenever the available labels were insufficient for certification. Blocking avoids deploying an uncertified model, but it does not guarantee that the fallback meets the same requirements: in our analysis no bedside score met both of them. A hospital adopting an external model should therefore decide in advance what care it will provide if the model is blocked.

**Planning label collection.** Septic patients are the binding resource. In our evaluated settings, local samples of a few hundred patients, with a few dozen septic cases, were often enough to block a badly broken model but rarely enough to certify a working one. The calculator lets a hospital estimate in advance whether certification is achievable with the labels it can afford. For the A→B transfer at the primary requirements, two estimates bracket the cost. CAB always splits the labelled sample in half, certifying the adapted model on one half. In a simulation that held fixed an adapted model fitted on the hospital's full training set, so that the fit half went unused, a total of about 6,000 labelled patients (about 340 septic, about 170 of them in the certification half) let CAB certify that model in 90% of samples. In the full experiment, where CAB refits the adapted model on the fit half of each sample, about 12,000 labelled patients (about 690 septic in total) were needed for the same 90%, consistent with the smaller fitting sample producing an adapted model that cleared the requirements by a smaller margin. These are estimates for that transfer and configuration, not a general minimum for CAB. The calculator is a planning approximation for data-selected thresholds, not a guarantee of the labels required.

**Tolerance and headroom.** In our same-hospital control, the hospital's false-alert tolerance had a substantially larger effect than the vendor's threshold: raising the tolerance from 0 to 10 points changed approval of the unchanged model at 8,000 labels from 4% to 98% of samples, whereas extra sensitivity headroom in the vendor's threshold changed approval by at most 3 percentage points. Hospitals should therefore state how many additional false alerts they will accept before labelling begins. Vendors can help most by publishing their full home operating curve, with confidence intervals, rather than a single tuned threshold.

**Order of the options.** The order saves labels. Testing the unchanged model first preserves the full labelled sample for its certification when no adaptation is needed, whereas an unordered procedure that reserves part of the sample for fitting has less independent certification data for that case. The same-hospital control showed the effect: CAB chose the correct remedy more often than the unordered procedure.

**Change control.** The gate fits existing change-control processes. A vendor could propose recalibration and local adaptation as pre-specified modifications in a predetermined change control plan or relevant technical documentation, where permitted by the applicable regulatory framework, with CAB's tests serving as proposed acceptance criteria and blocking as the fallback [@fda2024; @euaiact2024]. The gate's output, the option chosen with its confidence bounds, would then document the decision. This is our reading of the frameworks, not regulatory advice. Whether a hospital-run test could serve as a manufacturer's acceptance criterion is a question for regulators, a hospital's CAB result does not by itself satisfy a manufacturer's regulatory obligations, and CAB does not replace regulatory authorisation or clinical validation.

### 6.4 Limitations

- **Two hospitals, one country, one definition.** The Challenge data come from two US hospital systems, with a single, retrospectively applied Sepsis-3 definition. Larger multi-hospital databases such as eICU [@pollard2018] would allow many more transfers and different practice patterns to be studied.
- **Retrospective evaluation.** All the evidence is retrospective. The deployments were judged on held-out patients from the same records, not observed prospectively, and no effect on clinical decisions or patient outcomes was measured.
- **The definition of detection.** A septic patient counts as detected if alerted from 12 h before to 3 h after onset, so a detection is not necessarily an early warning; Section 4.8 reports separately how many patients were alerted at least 6 h before onset. The requirements also count patients rather than weighing how early an alert comes or the consequences of each missed case.
- **Finite evaluation populations.** Safety was judged on finite sets of held-out patients, so the measured unsafe-deployment rate is noisy in the settings with the fewest unlabelled septic patients. The per-setting intervals describe repeated sampling conditional on these two hospitals' data, not uncertainty over other hospitals or future patient populations.
- **Simulations with replacement.** The calculator check and the headroom and tolerance analyses resample patients with replacement and hold the models fixed. They describe sampling from the observed patients' distribution, not the evidence that the same number of distinct patients would provide.
- **Conservativeness.** The union bound and the exact tests make CAB conservative. Across the evaluated settings, the observed unsafe-deployment rates were at most about a fifth of the nominal level. This motivates investigating less conservative error allocations or alternative tests, but any such change would need its own theoretical justification to preserve the guarantee.
- **A research prototype.** The application has not been integrated with an electronic health record, evaluated for usability or validated for clinical use.

### 6.5 Two Variations of CAB

Two variations were evaluated with 100 runs per setting: the first in the whole-hospital transfers and the same-hospital control, the second in A→B. Changing the split of labelled patients to 30% for fitting and 70% for certification did not improve the share of correct remedies. A screen that skips Stage 1 when the share of alerted patient-hours on unlabelled data from the receiving hospital indicates a large shift, and then runs Stage 2 alone with its two tests at α/2, deployed the adapted model more often for A→B at the same budgets (in 25% of runs against 21%, averaged over budgets and requirement pairs), with unsafe deployments in 1.2% of all runs against 0.8% (with 100 runs in every setting, this equals the mean of the per-setting rates). Pooling all runs across settings, among those resulting in deployment, these rates were 4.9% and 3.6%, respectively. In these experiments the screen's decision was a fixed function of the unlabelled pool, made once before any label was drawn, and conditional on that decision the argument of Section 3.5 bounds the probability of an unsafe deployment by α. In practice the same holds only if the screen is computed before labelling on data independent of the labelled sample; a screen computed on data that overlap the labelled patients would need its own justification. Both variations are reported as empirical explorations, not as part of the method evaluated in Section 4.

### 6.6 Future Work

Four extensions follow directly. First, fewer labels. Prediction-powered inference [@angelopoulos2023] could potentially use the model's predictions on unlabelled patients to reduce the labels needed, provided the resulting procedure preserves the risk-control guarantee. Sequentially valid tests or confidence sequences could allow labelling to stop once the evidence is sufficient, without invalidating error control through repeated checking; repeatedly applying the fixed-sample tests used here would not. Second, a less conservative allocation of the error level between the stages than the equal split used here, with its own proof. Third, monitoring after deployment: combining the gate with sequentially valid monitoring of audited patients could extend a guarantee beyond the moment of deployment. Fourth, other settings: the same certification framework could potentially be extended to other imported classifiers with alert thresholds, such as automated electrocardiogram interpretation, provided that the corresponding requirements, sampling assumptions and finite-sample tests can be established, and to multi-hospital sepsis data in which many transfers can be studied.
