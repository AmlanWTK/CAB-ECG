## 2 Background and Related Work

This section reviews four bodies of work that bear on the deployment decision: sepsis early-warning systems and evidence on how they transfer between hospitals, methods for updating an imported prediction model, distribution-free risk control, and corrections for dataset shift that do not need local labels. It then outlines the regulatory context and positions CAB relative to these approaches.

### 2.1 Sepsis Early Warning and Its Transfer between Hospitals

Rule-based bedside scores are widely used to identify deteriorating patients. Examples include the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria, the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA), the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) and the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) [@rcp2017]. These scores are transparent and require no training data, but their accuracy for detecting deterioration in infected patients is modest. In a large cohort of ward and emergency patients with suspected infection, no score combined high sensitivity with high specificity, and the general early warning scores outperformed qSOFA [@churpek2017].

Machine-learning models trained on EHR data usually discriminate better at the hospital where they are developed. The PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2019 released hourly data from two US hospital systems so that algorithms could be compared on a common footing, and evaluated them on a third, undisclosed hospital system [@reyna2020]. Performance on the undisclosed hospital was lower, particularly for algorithms that had learned data-collection patterns specific to the hospitals they were trained on. Prospective evidence is similarly mixed. In a multi-site implementation study of the TREWS early warning system, prompt confirmation of alerts by a clinician was associated with lower mortality, although an observational association of this kind does not by itself establish that the alerts caused the difference [@adams2022]. An external validation of a widely implemented proprietary model, by contrast, found poor discrimination and low sensitivity at a new site [@wong2021]. A recent prospective evaluation of an updated version of that model at four US health systems reported good discrimination overall but substantial variation between institutions, a low positive predictive value and a heavy alert burden, and its authors recommended that every adopting hospital validate the model locally [@wong2026]. Evaluation design also affects these conclusions: models appear considerably more accurate when alerts raised after clinicians have already begun sepsis treatment are counted as correct predictions [@kamran2024]. In intensive care data from three countries, a deep-learning model lost discrimination when transferred to another database and recovered part of it after fine-tuning on a small share of the new site's patients [@moor2023].

Collectively, these studies establish that sepsis early-warning models must be evaluated at the receiving institution, and show that transfer can affect discrimination, sensitivity and alert burden. Evidence that performance has deteriorated does not, however, determine which deployment option a hospital should adopt, or whether its local data are sufficient to justify deploying any of them. This distinction motivates a deployment procedure that evaluates candidate configurations against prespecified requirements and permits non-deployment when compliance with those requirements cannot be certified.

### 2.2 Updating an Imported Prediction Model

Clinical prediction research distinguishes a hierarchy of updating methods for a model applied in a new population [@steyerberg2019; @vancalster2019]:

- recalibration in the large, which re-estimates the intercept;
- logistic recalibration, which re-estimates the intercept and the slope of the linear predictor;
- model revision, which re-estimates some or all of the coefficients;
- redevelopment of the model on local data.

In this literature, recalibration adjusts the relationship between predicted and observed risks, which is distinct from changing the decision threshold at which alerts are raised. In CAB, *calibration* denotes the latter operation only: selecting a new alert threshold while leaving the model and its predicted scores unchanged. This restricted definition reflects the deployment options evaluated in this chapter, in which the imported model is a tree ensemble and its requirements are stated for alerts at a threshold.

Vergouwe and colleagues proposed a closed testing procedure that moves up the updating hierarchy only when a likelihood-ratio test shows that the next level fits the local data significantly better [@vergouwe2017]. Sample-size guidance for external validation determines how many patients, and how many outcome events, are needed to estimate calibration and discrimination with a target precision [@riley2021]. These methods were designed for different questions from the one considered here. Closed testing selects an updating strategy on evidence of improved fit, and sample-size calculations address the precision with which performance can be estimated. Neither objective is equivalent to controlling the probability that the selected configuration violates prespecified deployment requirements, such as a minimum sensitivity at the alert threshold in use. CAB addresses this distinct decision by requiring statistical certification before deployment and permitting the model to be blocked when certification is unsuccessful. By contrast, the updating hierarchy selects an updating strategy on the basis of model fit; it does not itself provide a certification-based decision to deploy or block a configuration.

### 2.3 Distribution-Free Risk Control

Distribution-free predictive inference offers guarantees that do not depend on how well a model fits. Risk-controlling prediction sets [@bates2021] and Learn-then-Test [@angelopoulos2021] calibrate parameters of an arbitrary pretrained model, such as a decision threshold, so that chosen risks are controlled with high probability. They require only that the calibration data be exchangeable with the data on which the model will be used. Conformal risk control instead bounds the expected value of a monotone loss [@angelopoulos2024]. CAB requires a high-probability guarantee: that the population risks of the configuration it deploys satisfy the prespecified limits, except with probability at most α.

Learn-then-Test formulates certification as a multiple-testing problem. Each candidate parameter value is associated with a null hypothesis that its risk exceeds a prespecified limit, and rejecting that hypothesis certifies the value. The framework accommodates several risks at once and controls the family-wise error rate over the candidates. When a single risk is monotone in a scalar parameter, fixed-sequence testing can examine the values in order of that risk and certify them without a multiplicity penalty; this property depends on the monotonicity and on the validity of each individual test.

CAB builds on this framework in three respects, which Section 3 develops formally:

1. **Two patient-level requirements with a shared error budget.** Sensitivity is defined over patients who develop sepsis and the false-alert rate over patients who do not, so the two requirements are tested on disjoint groups of patients. Fixed-sequence testing selects the alert threshold from the septic patients, where sensitivity falls monotonically as the threshold rises; the false-alert test is then applied at that threshold using the non-septic patients.
2. **An ordered set of deployment options.** CAB evaluates options that differ in kind, not only parameter values of one model: keeping the imported model with its threshold, moving the threshold, and refitting the model. The order reflects the hospital's preference for the least intervention. It is not a statistical device. Validity across the options is established by allocating the overall error level α across four unsafe-deployment events: insufficient sensitivity or excess false alerts, for the imported model and for the adapted model. Each event receives an allocation of α/4, every individual test is carried out at level α/4, and a union bound over the four events gives an overall bound of α. The imported model's two false-alert tests, at the certified threshold and at the vendor's threshold, share one allocation: given the septic patients, at most one of the two can lead to an unsafe deployment. Section 3.5 proves this.
3. **An adapted model fitted and certified on separate patients.** Because adaptation changes the model itself, the labelled patients are divided before fitting, and the adapted model is certified only on patients it was not fitted on. Blocking is an explicit outcome when no option is certified.

### 2.4 Correcting for Shift without Labels

Label shift occurs when the distribution of outcomes changes between hospitals while the distribution of features within each outcome class remains stable. Under this assumption, the imported model's predicted probabilities can be corrected using unlabelled data from the receiving hospital [@saerens2002; @lipton2018]. Under covariate shift, the distribution of features changes while the distribution of the outcome given the features remains stable; training data can then be reweighted by the ratio of the target to the source feature densities [@shimodaira2000]. These corrections are well founded when their respective assumptions hold. However, differences in outcome prevalence or in feature distributions do not by themselves reveal which type of shift is present, and unlabelled data alone cannot establish whether the required assumptions hold at the receiving hospital. The corrections therefore do not, on their own, show that the corrected model meets prespecified sensitivity and false-alert requirements. We include a prevalence correction among the comparators to assess whether it is sufficient in this setting.

### 2.5 Regulatory Context

Regulators increasingly expect the changes a model may need after deployment to be planned in advance. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration's guidance on predetermined change control plans for AI-enabled device software allows a manufacturer to obtain authorisation for specified future modifications, together with the methods used to develop, validate and implement them and an assessment of their impact [@fda2024]. In the European Union, the Artificial Intelligence Act classifies as high-risk any AI system that is itself, or is a safety component of, a medical device requiring third-party conformity assessment [@euaiact2024]. For such systems, changes pre-determined by the provider at the initial conformity assessment and documented in the technical documentation do not constitute a substantial modification (Article 43(4)), and providers must monitor performance after the system is placed on the market. A 2026 amendment moved the date from which these obligations apply to devices covered by Annex I to 2 August 2028 [@euomnibus2026].

These developments emphasise defining model modifications, validation procedures and acceptance criteria in advance. CAB is consistent with that emphasis: the permitted options, the order in which they are tried, the evidence each must meet and the default when none passes are all fixed before any local label is examined. CAB is nevertheless a statistical procedure to support a hospital's deployment decision. It is not a substitute for regulatory authorisation, clinical validation or monitoring after deployment.

### 2.6 Positioning of This Chapter

Table 1 compares CAB with the approaches reviewed above by the local data each uses, its statistical objective and its implication for deployment. The approaches were designed for different purposes, and the table is intended to show which decision each addresses, not to rank them.

**Table 1.** Approaches to deploying an imported prediction model at a new hospital.

| Approach | Local data used | Statistical objective | Deployment implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deploy as supplied | None | None at the receiving site | Deploys the original configuration |
| Label- or covariate-shift correction [@saerens2002; @lipton2018; @shimodaira2000] | Unlabelled | Correct predictions under a specified type of shift | Deploys the corrected configuration |
| Local validation with point estimates | Labelled | Compare observed performance with the requirements | Depends on the acceptance rule; no error control |
| Sample-size guidance [@riley2021] | Labelled (planned) | Estimate performance with a target precision | Informs the planning of validation |
| Closed testing for model updating [@vergouwe2017] | Labelled | Select an updating strategy by tests of model fit | Selects an updating strategy |
| Risk-controlling prediction sets, Learn-then-Test [@bates2021; @angelopoulos2021] | Labelled | Certify parameter values of a fixed model against one or more risk limits | Certifies configurations of that model |
| CAB (this chapter) | Labelled | Bound by α the probability of deploying a configuration that violates either of two patient-level requirements | Keeps, calibrates, adapts or blocks |

CAB differs from these approaches in the decision it certifies. Rather than estimating performance, selecting an updating strategy on evidence of fit, or certifying parameter values of a fixed model, it evaluates an ordered set of deployment options against two prespecified patient-level requirements. It includes adaptation as a candidate option, certified on patients not used to fit it, and blocking as an explicit outcome when certification is unsuccessful. The resulting procedure links statistical risk control to the operational decision of whether, and in what form, an imported clinical prediction model should be deployed.
